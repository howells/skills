# Rhetorical Tells

Tells found in essays, newsletters, social posts and announcements, where the writer
stages a point before stating it. `signs-of-ai-writing.md` covers the encyclopaedic
register; this file covers the persuasive one.

The same caveats apply. These are clues, and a cluster counts where a single hit does
not. People wrote every one of these patterns before any model did. The quoted phrases
date quickly; the behaviour underneath lasts longer. In casual or spoken-register
writing, several of them are ordinary voice, so read the register before cutting.

Each entry gives the pattern, some watch phrases, and the default edit.

## Openers

### Throat-Clearing

A phrase that announces a point is coming.

- "Here's the thing", "Here's why", "Here's what I mean", "It turns out",
  "Let me be clear", "The real issue is", "Can we talk about".

Edit: delete the announcement and start at the point.

### Faux-Insight Setups

The writer claims to see what everyone else has missed, before saying anything.

- "What most people get wrong", "What nobody tells you", "The part everyone skips",
  "The thing no one is talking about".

Edit: cut the setup. If the claim reads as weak without it, make the claim more specific.

### Rhetorical Setups

A staged question or prompt in place of a statement.

- "What if I told you", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", "Spoiler:",
- a question answered by the next sentence: "The result? A 40% drop."
- unrequested permission: "And that's okay."

Edit: state the answer as a sentence. "Churn fell 40%."

## Sentence Structures

### Staged Contrasts

`signs-of-ai-writing.md` lists the "not just X, but Y" family. The same move turns up
in other forms:

- "X isn't the problem. Y is."
- "It feels like X. It's actually Y."
- "The question isn't X. It's Y."
- "It stops being X and starts being Y."
- "Not because X, but because Y."

Edit: state Y. Keep the X half only when a reader plausibly believes X.

### Negative Listing

Several denials before the claim: "Not a framework. Not a library. A protocol."

Edit: "It is a protocol."

### Colon Reveals

A noun phrase, a colon, then a short dramatic payoff: "The surprising part: nobody
had measured it."

Edit: write one ordinary sentence. "Nobody had measured it." Colons stay for lists,
labels and quotations.

### Dramatic Fragmentation

Fragments stacked for weight: "One file. One function. That's it. That's the whole
system."

Edit: a complete sentence. "The system is one function in one file."

### False Agency

An abstraction or object performs an act that a person performed, which hides who
did it.

- "the decision emerged", "the data tells us", "the market rewards",
  "the culture shifted", "the roadmap evolved".

Edit: name the actor. "The board decided in March." If the draft does not say who
acted, flag the gap; do not guess.

### Vague Declaratives

A sentence that states importance and withholds the content.

- "The implications are significant", "The reasons are structural",
  "The stakes are high", "This changes everything".

Edit: name the implication, the reason or the stake. If the draft has none, cut the
sentence.

## Commentary On The Prose

### Emphasis Crutches

A phrase that tells the reader how much weight to give the last sentence.

- "Let that sink in", "Full stop", "Period", "Read that again",
  "I'll say it again".

Edit: delete. If the sentence before needs the help, make it more specific.

### Interpretive Commentary

The writer steps outside the subject to tell the reader what to notice.

- "This matters because", "The key point is", "That matters more than it sounds",
  "This distinction is important", "As you can see", a redundant "In other words".

Edit: delete the label. If the reason is missing, supply it as a fact from the draft.

### Signposting

The text describes its own structure.

- "In this section we'll", "As we'll see", "Let me walk you through",
  "The rest of this piece explains", "Before we dive in".

Edit: delete. Reference documentation and long reports can keep a real signpost that
helps a reader find a section.

## Endings And Rhythm

### Quotable Closers

A final line, or the last line of a paragraph, that restates the point as an
aphorism, metaphor or mic-drop. "In the end, the tool was never the point. The
habit was."

Edit: delete it and end on the last concrete sentence. Do not replace it with a
better aphorism. If the piece needs closure, add a plain next step.

### Uniform Rhythm

- consecutive sentences with the same shape and length,
- every paragraph built setup, elaboration, punchline,
- every paragraph ending on a short emphatic line.

Edit: change a sentence only where the repetition is audible. Do not impose a
different pattern in its place.

## Word Level

### Filler Phrases

Cut when they delay the point.

- "it's worth noting", "it's important to note", "at its core",
  "at the end of the day", "when it comes to", "in today's world",
  "in a world where", "the reality is", "in terms of", "going forward",
  "in order to".

### Empty Intensifiers

- "really", "truly", "genuinely", "simply", "actually", "literally", "deeply",
  "fundamentally", "inherently", "incredibly".

Cut one when the sentence means the same without it. Keep one that carries contrast,
real uncertainty or the writer's spoken rhythm. Adverbs are cut when empty, never
wholesale.

### Workplace Jargon

| Written | Say |
| --- | --- |
| navigate the challenges | handle, deal with |
| unpack | explain |
| lean into | accept, do more of |
| double down | commit, increase |
| deep dive | analysis |
| circle back | return to |
| moving forward | next, from now on |

Vocabulary that clusters in generated marketing and product copy, additional to the
era lists in `signs-of-ai-writing.md`: leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, streamline,
empower, elevate, harness, unlock, realm, paramount, transformative, game-changing,
cutting-edge, ever-evolving. Judge these by density. One "robust" in a technical
document is ordinary English.

### Weak Verb Phrases

| Written | Say |
| --- | --- |
| made a decision | decided |
| has the ability to | can |
| is able to | can |
| provides support for | supports |
| conducted an analysis of | analysed |

## Formatting

- a heading over a section of one or two sentences,
- bullets where two sentences of prose would read better,
- emoji in headings or as bullet markers,
- bold scattered mid-sentence for emphasis.

Edit: let the format follow the content. Merge short sections and turn fragmentary
bullets back into sentences.

## The Portability Test

Move the sentence, unchanged, into a piece about a different company, person or
product. If it still fits, it says nothing about this one. Cut it, or replace it with
a fact, mechanism or consequence the draft already contains.

## Limits

These rules stop short of several absolutes, on purpose:

- Passive voice is fine when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.
- A sentence may open with "what", "when", "why" or "how".
- A list of three is fine when there are three things.
- A lone em dash, adverb or short sentence is ordinary writing.
- Second person suits instructions and is wrong for many other genres.
- Do not score a draft. Name the pattern, quote the line, give the edit.
